Inside an MRI

How an MRI scanner makes pictures of the inside of the body without X-rays. Step by step, I take you into the scanner suite: the machine and its parts, the journey from a proton to a picture, why one exam gives many pictures, a patient’s way through a scan, the safety built around a magnet that never switches off, and the people who make it work.

About this page: General principles only, for curious visitors, patients’ families and students: not operating instructions, and not medical advice. The drawings are my own and show no particular maker’s scanner. The links go to the publishers and official sources, and I earn nothing from them.

Five little cells, one of every color, signed up to be protons for the day. In the magnet’s field they all leaned, just slightly, the same way, and began to spin like tops, all to the same beat. Then a radio pulse arrived, a quick tap in time with their spin, and the five tipped to one side and sang back, very softly; the coils caught every note. All around them the scanner drummed and knocked. The patient, with earplugs in and music in the headphones, lay still and listened until the drumming sounded like a song. Between the sets of pictures, a friendly voice came through the speaker: "You’re doing really well. Just a few more minutes." When the last drumroll stopped, the pictures came out sharp and clear. The patient walked out into the daylight, and the five were already spinning again, ready for the next visitor.

Before you start

  • This is an educational overview of general principles only.
  • It gives no operating, quality-check or servicing instructions, and it is not medical advice: questions about your own scan belong with your care team.
  • Real procedures depend on the scanner and the site, and are set by the makers, the hospitals and each country’s rules.
  • Only qualified, trained and authorized people run these scanners.

What this tutorial does not teach

  • the controls, or how to run a scan
  • scan settings, or which scan suits which patient
  • how to screen a person, or whether an implant or a device may go into a scanner
  • how to carry out the scanner’s quality checks, or how its safety systems are tested
  • how to service or repair it, or how to handle its helium

And whatever you learn here, never go into an MRI scanner room, or take anything into it, unless the MR staff have checked you, and everything you carry, and invited you in: its magnet is always on.

Two small labels guide you: General principle, with its sources, for what holds for MRI scanners in general, and Varies by model or site, for what each scanner and each center does in its own way, which this tutorial leaves out on purpose.

1 MRI in one minute

Magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, makes detailed pictures of the inside of the body with a strong magnet and radio waves. It shows soft tissues, such as the brain, the spinal cord, muscles, joints and organs, especially clearly; for bone, CT is usually better. It uses no X-rays, and no ionizing radiation at all.

It reads hydrogen. Water and fat hold vast numbers of hydrogen atoms, and the core of each, a single proton, behaves like a tiny magnet. In the scanner’s field these tiny magnets lean very slightly toward the field. The lean is tiny, but across billions upon billions of protons it adds up to a faint magnetism along the field, called the combined magnetism. It turns like a wobbling top, about 64 million times a second in a 1.5 tesla scanner. A short radio pulse at exactly that rate tips it over; as it keeps turning, it sends a faint radio signal to coils near the body, then fades and recovers at rates that differ from tissue to tissue. Other coils give each point in the body its own rate, so a computer can tell where each signal came from and build a picture.

A scan takes about 15 to 60 minutes, sometimes longer. The patient lies on a table that slides into a tunnel and has to keep still. The scanner knocks loudly while it works, so everyone inside wears ear protection, and some people find the tunnel uncomfortably small. Accidents are very rare compared with the millions of scans done. But the magnet is always on, so every person and every object is checked before going into the scanner room.

2 Meet the machine

A scanner suite, drawn simply: no real suite or scanner looks exactly like this. Choose a part of the drawing to read about it, or show them all as a list.

  • Magnet

    The heart of the scanner, hidden in its round housing: a coil of special wire chilled by liquid helium to about −269 °C, where it loses all electrical resistance. The current then flows on by itself, so the magnet stays on day and night. Most hospital scanners are 1.5 or 3 tesla, tens of thousands of times stronger than the Earth’s magnetic field.

  • Bore and table

    The tunnel, and the table that slides the patient into it. The standard tunnel used to be 60 cm wide and is now 70 cm; the widest are 80 cm.

  • Gradient coils (hidden, just inside the bore)

    Three sets of coils that make the field a little stronger on one side than the other, so that each place in the body answers at its own rate: part 3 follows them. Switching them on and off very fast makes the scanner’s loud knocking.

  • Body coil (hidden, under the bore’s lining)

    Sends the radio pulse into the body.

  • Local coils

    Placed on or around the part being scanned, such as the head, a knee or the chest, to pick up its faint signal. The closer the coil, the clearer the picture, but each one sees only so deep.

  • Shims (hidden)

    Small steel pieces and extra coils that even out the magnet’s field, so that the pictures are not distorted.

  • Shielded room

    The scanner room is lined with metal, a Faraday cage, which keeps outside radio, such as radio stations, from drowning out the body’s faint signal, and keeps the scanner’s own radio in. The door seals all around, the window has a fine metal mesh, and pipes and cables pass through filters or narrow metal tubes that stop radio waves.

  • Warning sign and door

    A red sign at the entrance, lit even in a power cut, says the magnet is always on. The door stays closed, and preferably locked when no one is watching it, and only people the MR staff have checked and invited go through.

  • Control room

    Outside the scanner room: the staff run the scan from here, watch the patient through the window or on a screen, and talk with the patient over an intercom. The patient holds a call button, often a squeeze ball, to call them at any moment.

  • Equipment room

    Out of sight: the cabinets that power the gradient coils and the radio, and the water chillers that cool them.

  • Cold head

    A refrigerator on the magnet turns evaporated helium back into liquid, so that modern magnets lose almost none.

  • Quench pipe

    If the magnet ever loses its superconductivity, a quench, its helium boils off at once; this pipe carries the gas outside the building. Some newer sealed magnets hold so little helium that they need no pipe.

  • Oxygen monitor

    Warns, with a light and a sound, if escaping helium lowers the oxygen in the room.

3 From a proton to a picture

Follow the signal through a simplified scanner, from a proton to a picture, one step at a time.

1234567

Step 1 of 7

  1. 1

    Hydrogen gives the signal

    MRI listens to the hydrogen in the body’s water and fat. The core of each hydrogen atom, a proton, behaves like a tiny magnet.

  2. 2

    A tiny bias adds up

    Outside a magnet, these tiny magnets point every which way and cancel each other out, so together they add up to nothing. In the scanner’s field they lean very slightly along it, so slightly that only about one in a million is left over once the rest cancel out. But the body has billions upon billions of protons, so that leftover adds up to a faint but measurable magnetism along the field: the combined magnetism.

  3. 3

    It turns like a top

    The combined magnetism turns around the field like a wobbling top, at a rate set by the field’s strength: about 64 million turns a second at 1.5 tesla, and twice that at 3 tesla. One turn a second is one hertz, so that is about 64 megahertz (MHz) at 1.5 tesla and about 128 MHz at 3 tesla. Those are radio frequencies: FM radio broadcasts between 88 and 108 MHz, so 1.5 tesla sits just below the FM band and 3 tesla just above it.

  4. 4

    A radio pulse tips it

    The body coil sends a short radio pulse at exactly that frequency, the way a radio tuned to one station picks up only that one. The pulse pushes the combined magnetism away from the field’s direction, which runs along the tunnel, and tips it to one side, like a top knocked sideways by a small tap.

  5. 5

    Coils pick up the signal

    Tipped over, the magnetism keeps turning, and, like a magnet turning beside the coil of a bicycle’s dynamo, it makes a faint current in the coils near the body. That faint current is the signal the scanner picks up. Put simply, the pulse is what the scanner sends into the body, and the signal is the faint radio wave that comes back out afterwards.

  6. 6

    It fades, and recovers

    Two clocks then run at once: the signal fades, and the magnetism grows back along the field. Each tissue does both at its own pace, so tissues differ in brightness, and that is what makes a picture.

  7. 7

    Each place gets its own note

    The gradient coils make the field slightly stronger on one side, so each place turns at its own rate: one gradient picks a slice, and the others mark the positions within it. The signal that comes back is like a chord, and the computer separates it into its notes, with a method called a Fourier transform, to find where each part came from. It repeats this many times, then builds the picture. That is also why the patient must keep still: a picture made of measurements taken over minutes blurs if the body moves between them.

Go deeper

For readers who know a little about magnets and waves.

Why not “up or down”?

Many books say the protons in the scanner’s field can only point “up” or “down”, with slightly more up than down, and that the radio pulse “flips” them. MR experts warn that this is not what protons actually do. A more accurate description is that the combined magnetism turns like a top and the pulse can tip it by any angle, and this simple description predicts what is actually seen.

Superconductivity and helium

The magnet’s wire, usually niobium-titanium, carries current with no resistance only below about −264 °C, so it sits in liquid helium, which boils at about −269 °C. Conventional magnets hold about 1,500 to 2,000 liters of it; newer designs keep it from boiling away, and some sealed magnets need only a few liters, or less than one. “Helium-free” in advertising means sealed, with very little helium, not none.

Stronger and weaker magnets

Most hospital scanners are 1.5 or 3 tesla. Since 2017, 7 tesla scanners have been cleared in the United States for clinical scans of the head, arms and legs; at the other end, portable 0.064 tesla scanners need no cooling and plug into an ordinary wall socket.

Contrast agents

For some scans, a contrast agent, usually containing gadolinium, is injected into a vein. It makes the water near it recover faster, so the areas it reaches look brighter. Traces of gadolinium can stay in the body for a long time; regulators have not found that this harms people with healthy kidneys, but they restricted some types as a precaution, and the care team decides whether contrast is needed.

General principle

Faster pictures, and their limits

New ways of computing, artificial intelligence among them, can build good pictures from fewer measurements, so scans get shorter. Researchers warn that some can also add details that look real but are not there, so these methods are tested with care.

5 Many pictures, one exam

An MRI exam is not one picture but several sets, each made to show different tissues brightly. Here is the same simple drawing of a head, made two ways.

Which picture shows the fluid around the brain and spinal cord bright?

Each set shows different tissues brightly, so one exam usually has several.

Why is it so loud?

Tap to switch a gradient on and off.

The magnet itself is silent. The knocking comes from the gradient coils: switched on and off very fast in the strong field, they shake, like a loudspeaker. It can be louder than 100 decibels, louder than a pneumatic drill, a fire alarm or a concert, so everyone in the room wears ear protection, usually earplugs, with ear muffs or headphones over them where possible.

6 Your scan journey

Seven stages, from your doctor’s request to the results, each with the people who look after it.

Here they are mixed up: put them in order with the arrows, then check.

  1. Your doctor asks for a scan

    Who: your doctor

    A doctor decides that MRI can answer a question about your health and refers you; the results will go back to them.

  2. Safety questions

    Who: the MR staff

    You will be asked about pregnancy or breastfeeding, operations you have had, kidney problems, allergies, and any metal in or on your body: implants, a pacemaker, clips, metal fragments, especially near the eyes. You may be asked for a recent blood test of your kidney function (creatinine), in case the radiologist decides to use a contrast agent. Answer every question, even if it seems small: the staff decide what is safe.

  3. Leave metal outside

    Who: you, with the MR staff

    Jewelry, watches, hair clips, hearing aids, phones and cards stay in a locker, and you may change into a gown. The staff check you once more before the scanner room: the American College of Radiology asks that patients who are awake be checked at least twice.

  4. On the table

    Who: the radiographer or technologist

    You lie on the table, a coil is placed around the part being scanned, and pads help you keep still and keep your skin from touching the tunnel or other skin. You get earplugs, often with ear muffs or headphones over them, and the call button.

  5. The scan

    Who: the radiographer or technologist, from the control room

    The table slides into the tunnel, and the staff can see you and talk with you throughout. The scanner knocks in bursts, with pauses between the sets of pictures; keep still, and sometimes hold your breath when asked. If you need contrast, it is injected into a vein, usually after the first pictures. It takes about 15 to 60 minutes, sometimes longer.

  6. Going home

    Who: the MR staff

    Without medicine to help you relax, most people go back to their day straight away; if you had some, you will need someone to take you home. The team tells you what to do afterwards.

  7. Results

    Who: the radiologist

    A radiologist, a doctor who reads such images, studies them and writes a report for your doctor. How the results reach you varies from place to place.

Varies by model or site This is the usual journey. The details and the timing vary from center to center, and from patient to patient.

7 Safety by design

On the LINAC page, opening the door stops the beam. An MRI scanner’s magnet cannot be stopped like that: it is always on. So here the protection is the door itself, and the trained people who decide who and what may pass.

Four zones, one door

The American College of Radiology divides an MRI suite into four zones, like rings around the magnet; British guidance draws the same rings with other names. Try to take these people and things through, and see what the MR staff say at the door.

  • Zone I: public areas, open to everyone.
  • Zone II: reception and waiting, where people are asked the safety questions.
  • Zone III: a locked area with the control room, entered only with the MR staff.
  • Zone IV: the scanner room. A red sign says the magnet is always on.

At the door, the staff ask

  1. Checked and invited in?
  2. Label read?
  3. The maker’s conditions confirmed by the MR team?

Unknown means unsafe. And the door is for everyone: staff, visitors, cleaners and engineers too, not only patients.

A game about the door only: it is not screening advice, and real implants never appear in it. Whether a person or a device may go into a scanner is always decided by the MR team.

Three labels

Things made to go near a scanner carry one of three labels. The drawings are mine; the real marks are set by a standard, ASTM F2503.

  • MR Safe (a square with the letters MR: white letters on a green background, or green letters on a white background): no known hazards in any MR environment; made only of materials that are not metal, not magnetic and do not conduct electricity, such as a cotton blanket.

  • MR Conditional (a yellow triangle with the letters MR): shown to be safe only under the conditions its maker sets, such as the magnet’s strength; the MR team checks them against the scanner and the planned scan.

  • MR Unsafe (a red circle with a bar across the letters MR): an unacceptable risk in the MR environment, so it is kept out of the scanner room, such as a pair of scissors that a magnet attracts.

No label? Treat it as MR Unsafe. The old term “MR compatible” should no longer be used, because it was so often misunderstood.

Four hazards

  • The magnet pulls

    It attracts iron and steel strongly, more and more steeply near the tunnel, so an object that barely tugs at the door can fly in. Some implants can move or stop working properly. Metal that is not magnetic, such as aluminum or copper, is not pulled in, but if someone moves it quickly near the scanner, say by waving an aluminum sheet, it resists the movement as if it were in honey, because the movement sets up small electric currents inside the metal.

  • Radio waves heat

    Burns are the most often reported problem in the UK. The scanner’s radio waves drive an electric current around any closed loop that conducts: a cable lying in a loop, or the body itself, when the hands are clasped, the thighs touch, or skin touches a cable or a coil. Where the loop closes, the current crosses a small patch of skin, heats it and can burn it, and patients may not feel it at once, especially if they are sedated. So pads keep skin apart, patients are asked not to cross their arms or legs, and cables are laid so that they form no loops.

  • The gradients knock and tingle

    The noise can be louder than 100 decibels, so everyone wears ear protection. Fast switching can also make muscles or nerves twitch.

  • The helium

    In a quench, the helium boils off with a loud bang and a white cloud of cold gas, which is condensed moisture, not smoke. If it leaks into the room, the oxygen alarm sounds: everyone leaves at once, keeping low, and the door is propped open.

If someone collapses in the scanner

General principle

If a patient collapses, the trained staff stop the scan, call for help, start first aid and move the patient out at once, on a table or trolley that is safe there, to a place prepared beforehand, where resuscitation goes on. A defibrillator and other ordinary equipment never go into the scanner room. A quench is not the answer: it does not switch the field off at once. If moving the patient would take more than a few seconds, chest compressions start in the room. In Iran, inspectors check that an MRI department has resuscitation equipment suited to it.

What went wrong?

Three accidents, one for each of the scanner’s three fields: the magnet’s field, the radio waves and the gradients. Each shows a safeguard that was missing or not followed, not one person’s fault.

  • the magnet: United States, 2001

    A six-year-old boy, sedated for an MRI scan, died when a staff member brought a metal oxygen cylinder into the scanner room and the magnet pulled it through the air into the scanner, where it struck him. The hospital’s own review found a wide range of failures, including poorly trained staff, and the state fined it, also for not reporting a similar accident four years earlier, in which an oxygen tank struck the scanner but no patient was there. The safeguard: nothing passes the door until it has been checked; oxygen and monitors made for the scanner room; training; and reporting near misses.

  • the radio waves: United Kingdom, reported 2017

    Two patients under general anesthesia had scans of the spine, one of them about 90 minutes long. Afterwards each had a line of burns on the chest or belly, along the cables of the heart monitor, which had probably lain directly on the skin. Asleep, they could not say that it felt hot. The safeguard: cables off the skin, pads, no loops, monitoring made for the scanner, and checks on anyone who cannot report heat.

  • the gradients: Iran, reported 2013

    A young man had a 25-minute scan of the brain without earplugs or headphones. About an hour later his ears were ringing and his hearing was reduced; three months later it had not recovered. The safeguard: everyone in the scanner wears properly fitted ear protection, both kinds together where possible.

Accidents like these are very rare compared with the millions of scans done, and most can be prevented. In several reported deaths from objects pulled into scanners, the person who brought the object in was not the one in charge of the room: the door is for everyone.

8 Who checks the machine, and when

Varies by model or site A scanner that images patients is checked often, by people trained for it. The weekly and yearly checks below are the ones the American College of Radiology sets for the scanners it accredits; the others come from a related standard that the College wrote with the American Association of Physicists in Medicine, and from common practice. This is one example among several: which checks, and how, each center decides within its country’s rules and the maker’s instructions. In Iran, the technical director of a hospital or an imaging center answers for all of its equipment, even if others are responsible for carrying out some tasks. Quality-control tests and calibration must be done at set times and by the rules.

  • Before the first patient

    Medical physicists

    After the maker’s engineers have installed and calibrated the scanner, the shielding, the evenness of the field, every coil, the quality of the pictures and the safety devices are tested; the results become the baseline for every later check.

  • Every day

    The MR staff

    Every person and every object is checked before going into the scanner room. The American College of Radiology also recommends a detector that finds iron and steel, to help these checks, never to replace them.

  • Every week

    Radiographers

    A test object, a phantom, is scanned to check that the scanner’s tuning, the accuracy of its pictures and their fine, faint detail are unchanged.

  • Every year

    Medical physicists

    The most complete checks of the scanner, and a review of its safety: signs, access control, screening and helium safety.

  • After a repair or an upgrade

    Engineers

    The maker’s or the hospital’s engineers, with the medical physicist, make extra checks as needed, especially after a major upgrade, and every service is recorded.

9 Three ideas of MR safety

A simple way to remember what the safety rules ask for. It is not an official list from any one source.

  • Keep metal out

    Every person and every object is checked at the door, and anything without a label is treated as unsafe.

  • Control the energy

    The radio waves and the gradients are kept within strict international limits, the patient is padded and watched, and everyone wears ear protection.

  • Plan for the unexpected

    Emergencies are planned and practiced in advance: a collapse, a fire, a quench or a door that will not open.

10 Who licenses it in Iran

Varies by model or site

MRI uses no X-rays. In Iran, an MRI scanner is among the “levelled” capital equipment, with CT scanners and linear accelerators, whose installing, moving and scrapping follow a rule of the Minister of Health of 2015 (1394). A center needs the Ministry’s agreement in principle, then a permit to install the scanner, and uses it under an operating license from the university’s Deputy for Curative Affairs. Commissions set up under Article 20 of the 1955 (1334) law on medical affairs review such licenses, and moving the scanner later needs the permission of the Deputy for Curative Affairs too. The center’s technical director must be a radiologist.

IMED, Iran’s General Directorate of Medical Equipment, part of the Iran Food and Drug Administration (IFDA), registers the scanner itself, gives each installed device an ID number, and accepts an imported scanner only with the approval of one of six regulators, such as the US FDA. Hospitals and day clinics must employ a technical officer for medical equipment, licensed under the IFDA’s supervision. INSO, Iran’s national standards body, lists no valid standard for MRI scanners (searched in November 2026).

11 Who does what

An MRI department is a team. These are the people a patient meets, or is looked after by without meeting them.

  • Radiologist

    A doctor who reads the images and writes a report for the doctor who asked for the scan. Often leads the unit’s safety rules.

  • Radiographer or MRI technologist

    Asks the safety questions, places the patient and the coil, runs the scan and talks with the patient over the intercom. Also scans the phantom for the weekly checks. “Radiographer” in Britain, “technologist” in the United States.

  • Medical physicist or MR scientist

    Tests the scanner before its first patient and at least once a year, sets up and reviews the weekly checks, and advises on safety.

  • The MR safety team

    A medical director answers for safety overall; a safety officer, often a senior radiographer, runs it day to day; and a safety expert, often an MR physicist and sometimes from outside, advises. Titles and duties vary by country.

  • Nurse

    May place the small tube in a vein used for contrast.

  • Sedation or anesthesia team

    Sedates or anesthetizes patients, mainly young children, who cannot keep still, and watches them throughout.

  • The maker’s service engineer

    Installs and calibrates the scanner, carries out the specialist tests, and does the scheduled maintenance and the deeper repairs.

  • Medical-equipment engineer

    Keeps the scanner and the systems around it working: planned inspections and preventive maintenance, the chilled water, the room’s temperature and humidity, the helium level and the oxygen monitor, often with the maker’s service engineers for the deeper work, and a record of every intervention. In Iran, the hospital’s technical officer for medical equipment, usually a biomedical engineer, also reports quality problems and adverse incidents with devices to IMED, part of the IFDA, through its reporting system. Engineers are screened at the door like everyone else, and their tools are among the things it must stop: in one study of US reports, tools, and parts of the magnet itself left exposed during servicing, made up about one in six of the objects pulled into scanners.

And many more: receptionists, porters, cleaners trained for the MR suite, IT staff, and the managers who keep it all running.

12 Check your understanding

Eight questions on what you have read. Take your time: there is no clock, and nothing is recorded.

Question 1 of 8

Which atoms give almost all of the MRI signal?

The hydrogen in the body’s water and fat. The core of each hydrogen atom, a proton, behaves like a tiny magnet. Read that part again

Why is the scanner room lined with metal?

The signal is a faint radio signal, at frequencies close to FM radio, so the room is a Faraday cage that keeps outside radio out and the scanner’s own radio in. MRI uses no X-rays. Read that part again

Is the magnet switched off between patients, or at night?

The current in a superconducting magnet flows on by itself, so the magnet stays on day and night. That is why every person and object is checked before going in. Read that part again

What tips the combined magnetism over?

The body coil sends a short radio pulse at exactly the rate at which the combined magnetism turns, and that pulse tips it. The gradients do something else: they give each point its own frequency, so the scanner can tell where each signal came from. The table moving in takes the body into the field, where the combined magnetism forms, but does not tip it. Read that part again

What makes the loud knocking?

The magnet itself is silent; the gradient coils shake as they switch in the strong field, like a loudspeaker. Read that part again

An object has no MR label. How should it be treated?

Unknown means unsafe: it stays out until the MR team has shown otherwise. Read that part again

What does “MR Conditional” mean?

The conditions, such as the magnet’s strength, must match the scanner and the planned scan; the MR team checks them. Read that part again

If a patient collapses in the scanner, where is the patient resuscitated?

The patient is moved out at once to a prepared place; a quench does not switch the field off at once, and ordinary equipment never goes into the room. Read that part again

Choose an answer first.

13 Words and sources

Words in this tutorial

The words are listed in alphabetical order.

Bore
The tunnel of the scanner, in which the patient lies.
Coil
A loop of wire. In MRI, coils send the radio pulse, pick up the signal, or change the field from place to place.
Combined magnetism
The faint magnetism left along the field in the scanner, once the tiny magnets of most of the body’s protons cancel each other out. Physicists call it the net magnetization.
Contrast agent
A substance injected into a vein for some scans, to make some tissues look brighter. In MRI it usually contains gadolinium.
Faraday cage
A room or box lined with metal that keeps radio waves out, or in.
Gadolinium
A metal used in most MRI contrast agents.
Gradient coils
Coils that make the field a little stronger on one side than the other, so that each place answers at its own frequency.
MR Conditional
A label: shown to be safe only under the conditions its maker sets.
MR Safe
A label: no known hazards in any MR environment.
MR Unsafe
A label: an unacceptable risk in the MR environment, so kept out of the scanner room.
MRIMain term
Short for magnetic resonance imaging: a way of making detailed pictures of the inside of the body with a strong magnet and radio waves, with no X-rays. The machine is also called an MRI, or an MRI scanner.
Phantom
A test object scanned to check that the scanner works as it should.
Quench
The sudden loss of superconductivity in the magnet’s coil: its helium boils off and its field dies away, though not at once.
Radiographer, or MRI technologist
The person who runs the scan.
Radiologist
A doctor who reads the images and writes the report.
Superconducting magnet
A magnet whose coil, chilled by liquid helium, carries current with no resistance, so it stays on by itself.
Tesla
The unit of a magnetic field’s strength. Most hospital scanners are 1.5 or 3 tesla.
Zone
One of the four areas into which the American College of Radiology divides an MRI suite, from the public areas (I) to the scanner room (IV).
