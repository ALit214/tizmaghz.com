Inside an Ultrasound

How an ultrasound scanner makes live pictures of the inside of the body from echoes of sound, without X-rays. Step by step, I take you to the bedside: the machine and its probes, the journey from an echo to a picture, what ultrasound shows and what it cannot, a patient’s way through a scan, the two habits that keep it safe, and the people who make it work.

About this page: General principles only, for curious visitors, patients’ families and students: not operating instructions, and not medical advice. The drawings are my own and show no particular maker’s scanner. The links go to the publishers and official sources, and I earn nothing from them.

Five little cells, one of every color, live deep inside a patient, in a quiet round corner where nobody ever visits. One morning the patient lay down on a couch, a dab of cool gel landed on the skin above them, and a probe came to rest there. Then came a knock: a tiny pulse of sound, pitched far too high for any ear. “Anybody home?” The cell nearest the probe answered first, and its echo raced back, up through the probe and along the cable. One after another the others answered, each in its own color, the deepest one last. And there on the screen stood all five, larger than life, each exactly where it stands in the body: a clear picture, just what the doctor needed. The patient wiped off the gel and went home smiling, and the five settled back down, rather proud of their first portrait.

Before you start

  • This is an educational overview of general principles only.
  • It gives no operating, cleaning, quality-check or servicing instructions, and it is not medical advice: questions about your own scan belong with your care team.
  • Real procedures depend on the scanner and the site, and are set by the makers, the hospitals and each country’s rules.
  • Only qualified, trained and authorized people use these scanners.

What this tutorial does not teach

  • the controls, or how to scan
  • settings, or which scan suits which patient
  • how to read a scan, or what a finding means
  • how to clean or disinfect a probe, step by step
  • how to test, service or repair a scanner

And whatever you learn here, leave scanning, and reading a scan, to the people trained for them: what a scan shows depends on the skill of the hand that moves the probe and of the eye that reads the picture.

Two small labels guide you: General principle, with its sources, for what holds for ultrasound scanners in general, and Varies by model or site, for what each scanner and each center does in its own way, which this tutorial leaves out on purpose.

1 Ultrasound in one minute

An ultrasound probe sends tiny pulses of sound far too high-pitched to hear, millions of vibrations a second. The pulses travel into the body, and part of each one bounces back wherever tissues meet. The scanner times every echo: assuming sound moves at 1,540 meters a second in soft tissue, the time tells it how deep the boundary is, and the echo’s strength sets how bright the dot is. Line by line, many times a second, these dots build a live gray picture. Gel keeps out air, which would reflect the sound. Ultrasound uses no X-rays, but it cannot see through bone or gas.

2 Meet the machine

A scanner at the bedside, and one of its probes cut open, drawn simply: no real scanner looks exactly like this. Choose a part of the drawing to read about it, or show them all as a list.

  • Console on its cart

    The body of the scanner, on wheels, so that it can come to the bedside. It holds the electronics that send the pulses and receive the echoes, and the computer that turns the echoes into a picture.

  • Screen

    Shows the picture live, as it is made. While the scanner works, two small numbers on it stand for the energy the probe is sending: part 7 explains them.

  • Control panel

    The keys and knobs with which the person scanning chooses the probe and the kind of picture. What each of them does is for trained users, and this tutorial leaves it out.

  • Beamformer (hidden, inside the console)

    Decides the exact moment at which each crystal of the probe fires, and adds up the echoes the crystals receive. That is how the beam is aimed and focused with no moving part.

  • Probe holders

    Where the probes rest between scans, out of harm’s way: a probe is easily damaged by a fall or a knock.

  • Linear probe

    A flat face and a rectangular picture, for structures close to the skin, such as the thyroid or the breast.

  • Curved probe

    A curved face and a wide, fan-shaped picture, for the abdomen.

  • Phased probe

    A small face that fits between the ribs and sweeps a wide fan from there, for the heart.

  • Internal probe

    A slim probe that scans from inside the body, to look more closely at an organ: in the vagina or the rectum, or, on a thin tube passed down the food pipe, for a closer look at the heart.

  • Cable and connector

    Carry the signals between the probe’s many crystals and the console. The cable is easily damaged, for example under the scanner’s own wheels.

  • Gel

    Spread between the probe and the skin so that no air is trapped between them: air would send the sound straight back.

  • Crystals (inside the probe)

    A row of tiny piezoelectric elements, up to a few hundred in one probe. Each turns an electrical pulse into sound, and a returning echo back into an electrical signal.

  • Matching layer and lens (inside the probe)

    In front of the crystals. The matching layer eases the sound from the hard crystal into the soft skin, and the lens on the probe’s face focuses the beam.

  • Backing (inside the probe)

    Behind the crystals: it damps their ringing, so that each pulse is short.

The probe is the scanner’s most delicate part. Studies have found faults in a sizeable share of the probes in daily use, often unnoticed by the people using them.

3 From an echo to a picture

Follow one pulse of sound into the body and its echoes back out, from an echo to a picture, one step at a time.

1234567

Step 1 of 7

  1. 1

    A pulse of sound, too high to hear

    The crystals in the probe send a very short pulse of sound that vibrates millions of times a second. Human hearing stops at about 20,000 vibrations a second, so nobody hears it.

  2. 2

    Gel lets it in

    Air between the probe and the skin would reflect the sound. Gel fills the gap, so the pulse passes into the body.

  3. 3

    Boundaries send echoes back

    Wherever two tissues meet that carry sound differently, a small part of the pulse bounces back as an echo. The rest travels on, to meet deeper boundaries.

  4. 4

    Time tells depth

    The scanner measures how long each echo takes to come back. It assumes that sound travels at 1,540 meters a second, the average for soft tissue, so the time tells it how far away the boundary is. The pulse goes there and comes back, so the depth is half the distance it traveled.

  5. 5

    Strength sets brightness

    A strong echo becomes a bright dot, and a weak one a dim dot. One pulse gives one line of dots, from the probe downward.

  6. 6

    Line by line, a live picture

    The probe sends pulse after pulse, each along the next line, until the lines fill a picture. It builds many pictures a second, so movement shows as it happens, such as a beating heart.

  7. 7

    Doppler adds movement

    Sound that bounces off moving blood comes back at a slightly changed pitch. The scanner measures the change and paints the flow over the gray picture: usually red for blood moving toward the probe and blue for blood moving away, not for arteries and veins.

Go deeper

For readers who know a little about sound and waves.

Detail against reach

Higher-pitched sound has shorter waves and shows finer detail, but it fades sooner in the body, so it reaches less deep. Deep organs are scanned with lower pitch, and structures near the skin with higher. Most scans use sound of about 2 to 15 megahertz: millions of vibrations a second.

Three ways to show flow

Color Doppler paints the flow over the picture. Spectral Doppler draws the speed of the flow at one spot as a graph against time. Power Doppler shows where blood is flowing, even faintly, but not which way.

Other ways of scanning

M-mode follows a single line of the picture over time, to show fast movement such as a heart valve. A 3D scan builds a picture with depth, and 4D is 3D in motion. Elastography estimates how stiff a tissue is. And in a contrast scan, tiny gas bubbles injected into a vein make the blood echo strongly.

Inside the crystal

A piezoelectric crystal changes shape when a voltage is put across it, and gives a voltage when it is squeezed. So the same crystals can do both jobs: they send the pulse, then listen for its echoes.

Scanners in a pocket

Some scanners are little more than a probe that connects to a phone or a tablet, so ultrasound can go where a cart cannot. Their pictures may be less detailed and their functions fewer, and their users have to guard against overheating, loss and leaks of patients’ data.

5 What it shows, and what it cannot

Ultrasound is the scan that comes to the patient: a live picture at the bedside, without X-rays. Here is what it shows well, where it stops, and how even its false marks can be useful.

What it shows well

  • soft tissues and organs
  • fluid
  • movement as it happens, such as a beating heart or flowing blood
  • and the machine is small enough to come to the bedside

Where it stops

  • bone: sound does not pass through it, so the scanner cannot see behind it
  • gas: the air in the lungs or the bowel blocks the sound
  • depth: deep organs, and larger bodies, are harder to show clearly
  • the hand: the picture depends on the skill of the person moving the probe

Read the clues

The scanner assumes that sound travels in a straight line, bounces back once, always moves at the same speed, and fades at the same rate everywhere. Where the body breaks these rules, the picture shows marks that are not really there, and people trained to read scans use them as clues.

Four false marks

Each mark is drawn twice, in the body and on the screen: pink is the sound the probe sends, cyan is its echo, and a dashed white outline is something the screen shows that is not really there.

  • In the bodyOn the screen

    A shadow: A dark stripe behind something that blocks the sound, such as a stone, bone or gas.

  • In the bodyOn the screen

    A bright band: A brighter stripe behind fluid, such as a cyst: less of the sound was lost on its way through.

  • In the bodyOn the screen

    A ladder: A row of evenly spaced lines, one after the other, behind a strong reflector such as the surface of a healthy lung: the sound bounced back and forth between two strong reflectors, and each extra trip was drawn as a deeper line.

  • In the bodyOn the screen

    A mirror image: A copy of a structure on the far side of a strong, curved reflector, such as the diaphragm: the sound took a longer, bent path, and the scanner drew it as a straight one.

Here is the same simple drawing made two ways: a round shape, once as a stone and once as a pocket of fluid.

Which picture shows the stone?

A stone blocks the sound, so no echo comes back from behind it: a shadow. Fluid lets the sound through almost untouched, so what lies behind it looks brighter than the tissue beside it.

What is that whooshing sound?

Tap to switch the Doppler sound on and off.

The ultrasound itself is silent to us: its pitch is far above hearing. The whoosh comes from the Doppler measurement. Blood moving toward or away from the probe changes the pitch of the echo a little, and that small change happens to fall within the range we can hear. So the scanner can play it through a speaker, and the flow is heard as a whoosh.

6 Your scan journey

Seven stages, from your doctor’s request to the results, each with the people who look after it. Not all of them happen while you are there.

Here they are mixed up: put them in order with the arrows, then check.

  1. Your doctor asks for a scan

    Who: your doctor

    A doctor decides that ultrasound can answer a question about your health: to look into a symptom, to follow a condition, to guide a needle, or to check on a baby during pregnancy. The results will go back to them.

  2. Getting ready

    Who: the department

    Most scans need little or no preparation. For some you are asked to come with a full bladder, or not to eat for some hours beforehand. The department tells you what to do for your scan.

  3. In the room

    Who: the person who scans

    You lie on a couch beside the scanner, often in a dimmed room, and uncover the part to be scanned. Gel is spread on your skin.

  4. The scan

    Who: the person who scans

    The probe is moved over the gel with a little pressure. It may be slightly uncomfortable, but a scan through the skin does not usually hurt. For some scans a slim probe is used inside the body: you are asked for your consent first, the probe has a fresh cover, a chaperone is offered, and the scan stops whenever you ask. A scan usually takes between 10 and 45 minutes.

  5. Wiping off, and going home

    Who: you

    You wipe off the gel and get dressed.

  6. After you leave: the probe is cleaned

    Who: the person who scans, or the staff who clean the probes

    Before the next patient, the gel is wiped off, and the probe is cleaned, disinfected to the level its use demands, and looked over for damage. Part 7 explains why this step matters so much.

  7. Results

    Who: a doctor or the sonographer, depending on the country

    A report goes to the doctor who asked for the scan. Who writes it depends on the country: in the United Kingdom, usually the sonographer who did the scan; in the United States, a doctor or another licensed medical provider who interprets the pictures; in Iran, the doctor who did the scan. How the results reach you varies from place to place.

7 Safety by design

Ultrasound has no door to guard. No harm from the sound of diagnostic scans has been confirmed; the infections on record came from gel and probes that were not clean. So here safety is in the hands of the people who use the scanner, through two habits: scan only for a medical reason, with the least energy that gives the answer, and clean the probe to the level its last use demands.

Habit one: a reason, and the least energy

Ultrasound is energy, and in tissue it can do two things: warm it slightly, and form tiny bubbles of gas in body fluids or tissue. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) considers ultrasound generally safe when trained people use it prudently, and adds that the long-term effects of this warming and of these bubbles are still unknown. The American Institute of Ultrasound in Medicine (AIUM) knows of no independently confirmed harm to patients from today’s diagnostic scanners, used without contrast agents.

While it works, the scanner shows two small numbers on its screen. The thermal index is a rough guide to how much the sound could warm tissue, and the mechanical index to how likely the bubble effects are. They are estimates, not measurements. Trained users keep both as low as a good picture allows, and the scan as short as its question allows. The idea has a name, ALARA: as low as reasonably achievable.

For the same reason, regulators and professional bodies discourage scans made only for a keepsake picture or video of a baby; pictures from a scan that was needed anyway are fine. And the FDA says that heartbeat monitors for use at home should be used only by trained health care providers, when medically necessary.

Habit two: clean for where it has been

How clean a probe must be depends on what it last touched. A probe used on healthy skin is cleaned and given a low-level disinfection after every patient. A probe used inside the body, in the vagina, the rectum or the food pipe, gets a high-level disinfection after every patient and, wherever a cover can be used, a fresh one each time. A probe that touches sterile tissue, as in an operation, is sterilized, or at the least high-level disinfected and used in a sterile cover.

A cover does not replace disinfection: covers can have tiny holes, or tear. And cleaning always comes first, because gel and dirt left on a probe keep the disinfectant from reaching its surface.

The gel matters as much as the probe. Whenever a needle is involved, the gel must be sterile and from a single-use pack, because the needle can carry gel into the body. Gel from an ordinary bottle is not sterile, even when its label says it stops bacteria from growing.

These are the three classes of the activity Clean, disinfected or sterile?, where three ultrasound probes are among the things to sort.

A damaged probe

General principle

A probe with a cracked face or a damaged cable is disconnected, taken out of use and tested by trained staff: damage can let fluid in, and with it comes a risk of electric shock.

The people who scan need looking after too: in surveys, up to 9 in 10 of them said they scan in pain, and such strain injuries are becoming more common.

What went wrong?

Four events, each named only by its country and year. Each shows a safeguard that was missing at one step.

  • the gel: United States, 2021

    A gel that was not sterile, sold in bottles for many uses, turned out to be contaminated with bacteria, even in bottles that had not been opened; how that happened was never established. Patients got bloodstream infections, mostly after procedures in which ultrasound guided a needle, such as placing a line in a vein. Regulators in the United States, Canada and Saudi Arabia told users to stop using it at once. The safeguard: sterile, single-use gel for any scan before or during a procedure that pierces the skin.

  • the probe’s cleaning: Canada, 2017

    For one week, internal probes at a clinic may have gone back into use before their cleaning and disinfection were complete. The health authority contacted the patients and offered them tests. It judged the risk of passing on an infection to be close to zero. The safeguard: knowing, between patients, that a disinfected probe is ready, so that appointments never outrun disinfection.

  • a faulty probe: United States, reported 2020

    Before a heart operation, a scan with a probe passed down the food pipe showed only a trivial leak of a heart valve. But the pictures were poor and the probe was running warm, so the team suspected the probe. A second probe showed that the leak was severe, and the valve was replaced during the operation. The warm probe was a clue, not the proven cause. The safeguard: checking a probe’s quality before it is used on a patient.

  • home heartbeat monitors: Australia, 2024–2025

    After reports that parents-to-be may have been falsely reassured by heartbeat monitors used at home, and put off seeking care, Australia’s regulator reviewed these devices and removed from its register every one meant for use without a health professional. A state health authority then told clinicians to advise against them. An untrained listener can mistake the mother’s own heartbeat, or the sounds of the placenta, for the baby’s. The harm came from false reassurance, not from the sound. The safeguard: a trained ear, and contacting the maternity team without delay whenever there is a worry about the baby.

Infections linked to ultrasound are usually traced to one safeguard that was left out. How often they happen is not known: reports to regulators cannot be turned into rates, and only some outbreaks are ever written up.

8 Who checks the machine, and when

Varies by model or site A scanner used on patients is checked often, by people trained for it. The cards below follow Iran’s directives, set by IMED, beside the guidance of American and British bodies. They say what kind of check is made and who makes it, never how.

  • Before the first patient

    Medical physicists or engineers

    With the supplier, the scanner and every probe are tested and their electrical safety is confirmed. The results are kept as the baseline for every later check.

  • After every patient, and every day

    The person who scans

    The probe is cleaned and disinfected to the level its use demands, and the probe, its face, its cable and its connector are looked over. A damaged probe is reported at once.

  • Every week

    The medical-equipment unit

    In Iran, IMED’s directive asks the center’s medical-equipment unit to check each week the probes’ condition and cleanliness, the cables and the scanner’s body, and to check on a picture that every crystal works. Elsewhere, each department sets its own interval for such routine checks, from every week to every six months.

  • Every year

    A quality-control provider, or a medical physicist

    A full test of the scanner and of every probe, with their electrical safety. In Iran it is made at least once a year by a quality-control provider that IMED has approved; in the United States and the United Kingdom, usually by a medical physicist, beside the service engineer’s planned maintenance.

  • At the end of its life

    The center’s managers and engineers

    WHO gives 5 to 10 years as an ultrasound scanner’s expected life, only as a rough guide: its use, its surroundings, spare parts and service support all decide how long it lasts.

Guidance differs on how often the probes themselves should be tested: once a year in some, at least every three months in others.

10 Who licenses it in Iran

Varies by model or site

In Iran, a scanner in a radiology institute or an imaging center is licensed as part of radiology: the Ministry of Health’s bylaw of 2024 (1403) on these centers counts imaging with ultrasound as radiology, so the scanner works under the center’s licenses. As for MRI, these come from the Ministry or the university’s Deputy for Curative Affairs, after review by the commissions set up under Article 20 of the 1955 (1334) law on medical affairs, and the center’s technical director must be a radiologist. In Iran, the radiation work permit from the Atomic Energy Organization is for machines that give off ionizing radiation, such as X-ray machines; ultrasound gives off none.

IMED, Iran’s General Directorate of Medical Equipment, part of the Iran Food and Drug Administration (IFDA), registers the scanner and sets the directives for its upkeep: the checks of part 8 come from them. Every medical institution names a technical officer for medical equipment, and faults and incidents with devices are reported to IMED. INSO, Iran’s national standards body, has Persian editions of the international safety standard for ultrasound scanners, IEC 60601-2-37; they follow its edition of 2007, one of them with its amendment of 2015; in 2024 IEC replaced it with a new edition, which INSO has not yet published in Persian.

Alongside the international rules, Iran has some national rules of its own for ultrasound scans; the Persian page names them.

11 Who does what

A scan takes more hands than the two that hold the probe. These are the people a patient meets, or is looked after by without meeting them.

  • Radiologist

    A doctor who specializes in imaging. In Iran, radiologists do the scanning themselves and write the report.

  • Sonographer

    In many countries, the person who does the scan: a professional trained in ultrasound, who in the United Kingdom usually writes the report as well. In Iran doctors do the scanning, and the rules I read name no such profession.

  • Doctors who scan in their own field

    Obstetricians, cardiologists and emergency doctors, among others. They are expected to be trained for the scans they do.

  • Medical physicist

    Tests the scanner and its probes before their first use and every year, and plans the routine checks and keeps their records. In Iran, the yearly test is made by a quality-control provider approved by IMED.

  • The staff who clean and disinfect the probes

    Usually the people who scan, following the infection-control rules: after each patient they remove the cover and the gel, look the probe over, and clean and disinfect it to the level its use demands.

  • Nurse or assistant

    Helps the patient get ready, and may stay as a chaperone. In Iran, a technician prepares the patient and, for some scans, types the report and hands it over.

  • The maker’s service engineer

    Maintains and repairs the scanner and its probes under contract. A poor repair of a probe can lead to a wrong or a missed diagnosis, so who may repair one matters.

  • Medical-equipment engineer

    Looks after the scanner between the service visits: the weekly checks of the probes, the cables and the body, taking a damaged probe out of use, the records of every check and repair, and the advice to repair or replace. In Iran this is the work of the center’s medical-equipment unit and of its technical officer, a post every medical institution must fill.

And many more: receptionists, porters, cleaners, IT staff, and the managers who keep it all running.

12 Check your understanding

Eight questions on what you have read. Take your time: there is no clock, and nothing is recorded.

Question 1 of 8

Does an ultrasound scan use X-rays?

Ultrasound makes its pictures from echoes of sound. It uses no X-rays, and no ionizing radiation at all. Read that part again

Why is gel put between the probe and the skin?

Air between the probe and the skin would send the sound back. Gel fills the gap, so the pulse can pass into the body. Read that part again

How does the scanner know how deep a boundary is?

It times each echo and assumes that sound travels at 1,540 meters a second in soft tissue: an echo that takes twice as long comes from twice as deep. Read that part again

What blocks ultrasound?

Sound does not pass through bone, and gas blocks it, so the scanner cannot see behind them. Fluid lets sound through easily. Read that part again

What is the whooshing sound heard during some scans?

Nobody can hear ultrasound. The whoosh is the small change of pitch that moving blood gives to the echo, the Doppler effect: it falls within the range we can hear. Read that part again

Which two habits keep ultrasound safe?

Ultrasound has no door to guard. Its safety is in the hands of the people who use the scanner: scan only for a medical reason, with the least energy that gives the answer, and clean the probe to the level its last use demands. Read that part again

An internal probe was used under a cover. What does it need before the next patient?

A cover does not replace disinfection: covers can have tiny holes, or tear. Read that part again

In Iran, who checks the probes and the cables every week?

IMED’s directive asks the medical-equipment unit to check them each week. The person who scans looks the probe over after every patient, and a full test is made at least once a year. Read that part again

13 Words and sources

Words in this tutorial

The words are listed in alphabetical order.

ALARA
Short for “as low as reasonably achievable”: the least energy, for the shortest time, that gives the answer.
Backing
The layer behind the crystals of a probe, which damps their ringing so that each pulse is short.
Beamformer
The electronics that time the firing of each crystal and add up their echoes, to aim and focus the beam.
Contrast scan
An ultrasound scan in which tiny gas bubbles, injected into a vein, make the blood echo strongly.
Doppler effect
The change of pitch of a sound that comes from, or bounces off, something moving. Ultrasound uses it to show blood flow.
Echo
Sound that bounces back from a boundary. An ultrasound picture is made of echoes.
Elastography
A kind of ultrasound scan that estimates how stiff a tissue is.
False mark
A mark in the picture that does not match what is really there, such as a shadow behind a stone. Specialists call it an artifact.
Gel
Spread between the probe and the skin to keep air out, so that the sound can pass.
High-level disinfection
The most thorough level of disinfection, one step below sterilization; probes used inside the body need it after every patient.
Matching layer
The layer in front of the crystals of a probe, which eases the sound into the skin.
Mechanical index
A number on the scanner’s screen: a guide to how likely the sound is to disturb tiny pockets of gas in tissue.
Megahertz
A million vibrations a second. Most scans use sound of about 2 to 15 megahertz.
Piezoelectric crystal
A material that changes shape when a voltage is put across it, and gives a voltage when it is squeezed. In a probe it sends the sound and receives the echoes.
Probe
The part held against the body, which sends the sound and receives the echoes. Engineers also call it a transducer.
Probe cover
A thin single-use sleeve put over a probe that is used inside the body. It does not replace disinfection.
Radiologist
A doctor who specializes in imaging.
Sonographer
A professional trained to do ultrasound scans and, in some countries, to report them.
Thermal index
A number on the scanner’s screen: a rough guide to how much the sound could warm tissue.
UltrasoundMain term
Sound too high-pitched for people to hear, above about 20,000 vibrations a second; and the way of making live pictures of the inside of the body from its echoes, with no X-rays. The scan is also called sonography, and the machine an ultrasound scanner.
