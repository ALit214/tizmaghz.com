Research

Laser therapy and stem cells for diabetic wounds

This experimental study in rabbits with diabetes examined two wound treatments: low-level laser therapy, which uses low-power laser light, and human stem cells taken from fat tissue (adipose-derived stem cells). Each was used alone and in combination. After 30 days, the wounds that received both treatments healed better, and the repaired skin was stronger.

TizMaghz A, Shojaei S, Tehrani P. The effect of combined low-level Nd:YAG laser with mesenchymal stem cells in the healing of diabetic wounds: an experimental study. Archives of Dermatological Research. 2025;317:317. doi:10.1007/s00403-024-03714-6

Build a tissue

Tissue engineering combines cells, a scaffold, and biological signals to build tissue that can repair or replace damaged tissue. Add the three ingredients one at a time to build a tissue.

0 of 3 ingredients added

  1. Cells

    Cells are the building blocks of tissue. Researchers can guide stem cells toward becoming specific cell types, then grow them on a scaffold.

  2. Scaffold

    The scaffold is the structure cells grow on. It can be built from many materials, from proteins to plastics, and it also relays the signals that guide the cells.

  3. Donor scaffold

    Some scaffolds come from donor organs: the organ’s cells are removed, and the remaining collagen framework is used to grow new tissue.

  4. Signals

    Biologically active molecules, such as growth factors, act as signals. Each signal can start a chain of responses that shapes what happens to a cell.

Engineered tissue

Together, cells, a scaffold, and signals can form functional tissue. Artificial skin and cartilage are engineered tissues approved by the U.S. FDA; more complex organs are still a long way from being ready for patients.

Now let’s build another tissue: a blood vessel.

Build a blood vessel

Add the four steps to grow a blood vessel. Some steps have to wait for others.

0 of 4 steps done

  1. Smooth muscle cells

    These cells make up much of the wall of a blood vessel. They come from a donor’s aorta, the body’s largest artery, and are multiplied in the lab.

  2. Tube-shaped scaffold

    The cells are seeded onto a mesh tube, so the new tissue grows in the shape of a vessel.

  3. Bioreactor

    A bioreactor recreates conditions like those in the body. As the cells grow, they lay down a framework of proteins around them, the extracellular matrix.

  4. Cells removed

    Finally, the cells and their genetic material are removed, as with a donor scaffold. What remains is a tube of human proteins that keeps its structure and strength.

Engineered blood vessel

In December 2024, the U.S. FDA approved the first blood vessel made this way. It is used in adults whose injured arm or leg artery must be repaired urgently to save the limb, when a vein of their own can’t be used.

Sources: U.S. National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering (NIBIB); U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)

How a wound heals

When the skin is wounded, the body repairs it in four stages. Move the slider to follow them.

  1. Hemostasis
  2. Inflammation
  3. Proliferation
  4. Remodeling

  1. Hemostasis: stopping the bleeding

    The body first works to prevent further blood loss. This starts within half an hour of the injury and can take up to a few days.

  2. Inflammation: defending the wound

    The immune system goes on high alert to protect the wound from infection. This usually lasts several days.

  3. Proliferation: new tissue

    The body produces new, healthy tissue to fill the wound.

  4. Remodeling: long-term repair

    Building on the earlier stages, the body works toward lasting healing of the area. This starts in the early weeks and can take up to a year.

Source: Essentia Health, a medically reviewed article on the stages of wound healing

The life of a medical device

Medical equipment needs managing from the first plan until the day it leaves service. Step through the five stages.

Stage 1 of 5

  1. Needs assessment

    Identify and prioritize the devices a facility needs by comparing what it has with what its services, patients, staff, and budget require.

    Source

  2. Acquisition

    Plan the purchase, including its costs, and choose a device and accessories that meet the agreed requirements.

    Source

  3. Introduction into service

    After delivery, the device is installed, commissioned, acceptance-tested, and calibrated. It is then recorded in the equipment inventory, and staff are trained to use and look after it.

    Source

  4. Maintenance

    Planned inspections confirm the device works correctly and is safe for patients and staff. Preventive maintenance extends its life and reduces failures; corrective maintenance fixes faults.

    Source

  5. Retirement

    When a device becomes obsolete, unusable, or no longer needed, it is decommissioned: removed from its original use, either for another use or for disposal.

    Source

Source: World Health Organization, Medical device technical series

Repair or replace?

Deciding when a device should leave service is part of managing it. What would you do?

  • A new model of your ultrasound machine has just come out. Yours works well, and the supplier still services it.

    Keep it in service. WHO warns that a new model reaching the market is not, by itself, a reason to call a device obsolete.

  • The supplier no longer services a patient monitor, and no spare parts or local technical support are available.

    Decommission it. WHO lists a shortage of technical support, spare parts, accessories, or consumables among the reasons to decommission a device.

  • A device has a safety problem that cannot be resolved.

    Decommission it. Unresolved safety issues are one of WHO’s reasons for decommissioning a device.

  • Your unit has replaced an older device, but the old one is still safe and reliable.

    Re-use it elsewhere. WHO notes that a device that is still acceptable and reliable may be reassigned, for example to a less demanding use, or donated when safety and performance conditions can be met.

Source: World Health Organization, Decommissioning medical devices (2019)

Sort the equipment

Regulators group medical devices by risk. In the United States, the FDA puts each type of device in Class I, the lowest risk, Class II, or Class III, the highest risk, which needs the strictest review before sale. Other countries use other systems: Iran, for example, has four classes, from A (lowest risk) to D (highest), following international guidance. Which FDA class is each device in?

  • Stethoscope (not electronic) Class IIt has no power and only rests on the skin, so its risk is low, even though it is a symbol of medicine. Source
  • MRI scanner Class IIIt is large and costly, but the class follows the risk to the patient, not the size or price of a device. Source
  • Implanted pacemaker Class IIIIt is implanted and keeps the heart beating, so it needs the FDA’s strictest review. Source
  • Condom Class IIIf it fails, the result can be a pregnancy or an infection, so beyond the basic rules it must meet extra requirements, such as standard tests. Source
  • Tongue depressor Class IA plain stick that touches the mouth for a moment: low risk. It needs only the FDA’s basic rules, with no review before sale. Source
  • Automated external defibrillator (AED) Class IIIIt stays outside the body, but it must work when a life depends on it, so it needs the FDA’s strictest review. Source
  • Manual wheelchair Class IMoved by hand, by its user or a helper, it carries a low risk. Source
  • Powered wheelchair Class IIIts motor, battery and controls add risks a manual wheelchair doesn’t have, so it must meet extra requirements, and the FDA reviews it before sale. Source

From lab to clinic

A new treatment is tested step by step, from the laboratory to everyday use. Put the steps in order, then see what a single result can and cannot tell us.

0 of 7 steps in place

Which comes first?

Done: all 7 steps are in place.

  1. 1

    Lab studies

    Could the idea work at all?

    Research on a new treatment begins in the laboratory, for example with cells. Source

  2. 2

    Animal studies

    Is it promising, and safe enough to try in people?

    Studies in animals, such as mice or rats, show whether an idea looks promising; others look for harm and help choose safe doses. They help decide whether to test it in people, but they cannot show that it is safe or works in humans. Source

  3. 3

    Phase one trial

    Is it safe in people?

    Researchers give it to a small group of people, about 20 to 80, for the first time, to learn about its safety and side effects. Source

  4. 4

    Phase two trial

    Does it work?

    Researchers give it to a larger group, about 100 to 300 people, to see how well it works and to study its safety further. Source

  5. 5

    Phase three trial

    How does it compare with the usual treatment?

    Researchers give it to large groups, about 1,000 to 3,000 people, to confirm how well it works, watch for side effects, and compare it with standard treatments. Source

  6. 6

    Regulatory review

    Is it safe and effective for its intended use?

    A regulator, such as the U.S. FDA, examines all the evidence and decides whether to approve the treatment for that use. Other countries and regions have their own regulators. Source

  7. 7

    Phase four (After approval)

    Is it still safe in everyday use?

    Researchers keep tracking its safety in the general population, because even large trials cannot show everything about a treatment’s safety before approval. Source

What can this result tell us?

These results are invented. Pick the best answer for each.

  • A new medicine lowered blood pressure in rats.

    It may be worth testing in people. A promising result in animals can be a reason to test a medicine in people. Only studies in people can show whether it is safe and works in humans. Source

  • In a phase one trial, 40 people took a new medicine and had no serious side effects.

    It looks safe enough to test further. Phase one trials are small and look at safety and side effects. Whether a medicine works is studied in phases two and three. Source

  • A regulator approved a new medicine for high blood pressure after a phase three trial in 2,000 people.

    Its safety will still be monitored. Even large trials cannot reveal everything about a medicine’s safety, so researchers keep tracking it after approval, in phase four. Source

Sources: U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH); U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)

Myth or fact?

  • Low-level laser therapy works by heating the tissue.

    Myth. Low-level laser therapy, also called photobiomodulation, is defined as a non-thermal process: light-sensitive molecules in cells absorb the light and set off chemical changes.

  • Most stem cell treatments sold by clinics are approved by regulators.

    Myth. In the United States, for example, the FDA has approved only a few such products, each for a named use, such as blood-forming stem cells from umbilical cord blood for disorders that affect blood production. It warns that other products are often marketed illegally.

  • Some scaffolds for new tissue are made from donor organs.

    Fact. The donor organ’s cells are removed, and the remaining collagen framework is used to grow new tissue.

  • Diabetes can make some sores harder to heal.

    Fact. Diabetes can lower blood flow in the feet, and poor blood flow can make it hard for a sore or an infection to heal.
